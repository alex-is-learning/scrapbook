## 3. "We can control the universe!" vs "follow the Dao"
- I'm just gonna turn to ChatGPT for this one, it's late

A deep, often unspoken assumption in rationalism and effective altruism is that the universe is, in some important sense, _fixable_: that with enough intelligence, information, coordination, and effort, we can understand what matters most and then deliberately steer reality toward better outcomes. This orientation treats the world as a problem space—complex, yes, but ultimately amenable to optimisation and control. Moral urgency follows naturally: if suffering can be reduced and futures improved through correct intervention, then failing to act decisively is not humility but negligence. From this perspective, confidence in models, planning, and leverage is not arrogance but responsibility.

Post-rationalist critiques tend to target not the _aim_ of care or improvement, but the _stance_ toward reality that accompanies this assumption. Drawing from traditions like Daoism, phenomenology, and systems thinking, post-rationalism emphasises that the world is not merely complicated but fundamentally _opaque_, self-organising, and responsive in ways that resist full representation. Attempts to “fix” or control it too directly risk Goodharting, unintended consequences, and the flattening of values that cannot be easily formalised. The Daoist-inflected warning here is not “do nothing,” but “act in accordance with the grain of reality”: cultivate attunement, timing, and responsiveness rather than imposing abstract plans. Where rationalism fears passivity, post-rationalism fears hubris—the belief that our models entitle us to command outcomes rather than participate humbly in unfolding processes.

Seen this way, the tension is less about optimism versus pessimism, and more about _how_ agency is understood. Rationalism foregrounds agency as intervention: selecting actions that cause desired effects. Post-rationalism foregrounds agency as participation: shaping oneself and one’s context so that appropriate action emerges from alignment rather than domination. The disagreement is not whether humans should care or act, but whether wisdom lies primarily in sharper maps and stronger steering, or in deeper perception and wiser yielding. The unresolved question—and perhaps the productive one—is how to act powerfully without mistaking the map for the territory, and how to follow the Dao without turning humility into an alibi for inaction.

“We Space” practices like Circling operate in a domain that sits largely outside the value structure of modern rationalist or “Orange” worldviews, which helps explain why they often feel confusing, trivial, or even suspicious to people grounded there. Orange tends to value clarity, propositional insight, individual competence, and outcomes that can be tracked, transferred, or scaled. Knowledge is something you _have_; growth is something you can explain; progress shows up as better models, skills, or results. From that frame, a practice whose “output” is a shift in shared attention, relational field, or moment-to-moment felt sense can look like noise, indulgence, or group therapy without a theory of change.

We Space practices work on a different assumption: that some of the most important human capacities—trust, mutual intelligibility, emotional regulation, collective sense-making—do not live primarily inside individuals, nor can they be accessed through detached analysis. Instead, they emerge _between_ people, in real time, through attention to micro-signals like tone, timing, posture, and affect. Circling is not trying to generate beliefs or conclusions; it is training sensitivity to how meaning is co-created in interaction. From within the practice, insight arrives not as a statement (“I learned X”) but as a felt reorganisation of how others show up and how one is able to respond to them.

This mismatch is why explanations often fail. To someone who values explicit models and instrumental leverage, saying “this helps you sense the relational field” is like explaining music theory to someone who has never heard sound. The practice is doing work, but it is not the kind of work that Orange has learned to recognise as real. Post-rational framings try to bridge this by pointing out that We Space practices cultivate capacities that Orange implicitly relies on but rarely trains—such as navigating ambiguity, repairing breakdowns, or coordinating without coercion. Still, your mentor’s point holds: if a culture has not yet learned to value participatory or relational knowing, We Space will feel unintelligible, not because it is nonsense, but because it is operating in a dimension of value that has not yet come online.
## Authentic relating

ChatGPT: "could you write a similar thing re: authentic relating? a sense that rationalists may mostly want to communicate for knowledge exchange, rather than like... emotional connection, vibes, something like that? (this is a crazy strawman I'm sure) "

You’re right to flag the strawman risk here — but there _is_ a real pattern underneath it, and it can be described without pathologising either side.

---

Authentic relating practices tend to foreground communication as a way of _being-with_ others, rather than primarily as a means of exchanging information. The point is not to transmit correct beliefs or converge on shared models, but to make present one’s lived experience—emotions, bodily signals, uncertainties, intentions—and to let those be received and responded to in real time. Meaning arises less from what is said than from how it is said and how it lands: tone, pacing, vulnerability, and mutual attunement matter as much as semantic content. From within this frame, connection itself is not a side-effect of good communication; it is the medium in which understanding becomes possible.

  

By contrast, many rationalist spaces implicitly treat communication as _epistemic plumbing_: a tool for sharing beliefs, arguments, evidence, and updates to one’s world-model. Clarity, compression, and accuracy are prized; affect is often seen as noise unless it carries informational value (e.g. as a bias to be corrected). In such a culture, conversations oriented toward “vibes” or emotional disclosure can feel inefficient, self-indulgent, or even manipulative, because they do not obviously advance the collective project of knowing more or deciding better. Importantly, this is not because rationalists lack emotional lives, but because their norms have learned to route legitimacy through cognition rather than through felt relational presence.

  

Authentic relating can therefore feel illegible or threatening in rationalist contexts: it asks participants to attend to channels of meaning they have not been trained to trust, and to value outcomes—like increased intimacy, resonance, or mutual safety—that cannot be easily audited or justified. From a post-rational perspective, the claim is not that knowledge exchange is insufficient, but that it rests on unspoken relational preconditions: trust, goodwill, emotional regulation, and the capacity to stay present during disagreement. Authentic relating practices aim to make those preconditions explicit and trainable. The tension arises when a culture that defines “real communication” as information transfer encounters a practice that insists that how we relate is not ancillary to truth-seeking, but constitutive of it.
