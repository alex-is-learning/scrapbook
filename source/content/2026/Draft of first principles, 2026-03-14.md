## Draft principles

Here are four. I think four is right — three loses too much, five starts to blur.

### 1. Walk into chaos. Make it legible.

This is what you do. It's what every manager described. It's what the scrapbook is. It's what consensus-ism is. It's what you're doing right now with first principles.

**The decision test:** _Does this situation need someone to walk into a mess and make it structured, clear, and visible?_ If yes, it's probably for you. If the structure already exists and you'd be maintaining it, you'll leave within 6 months.

**What it helps you say no to:** Defined roles with clear playbooks. Jobs where the org chart is settled and the processes already work. Maintenance. You'll be bored and you'll scrunching your way through it.[^1]

**What it helps you say yes to:** Early-stage companies. Undefined mandates. Anything where someone says "we don't really know what this role is yet." The thing Ethan described: _"Very little specificity in what he was supposed to do. Time after time, found increasingly critical areas to own."_

**The tension to watch:** This principle can become an excuse to never persist past the exciting early phase. Person 2 (the VC you admire) presumably stuck with venture capital long enough to actually get wealthy. There's a version of this principle that reads "leave the moment it's hard" rather than "leave when the crystallisation work is genuinely done." The honest check: _Am I leaving because the real work here is finished, or because the dopamine of novelty has worn off?_

**The decision test:** _Does this let me be entertaining, charismatic, and visible — at least some of the time?_ A job that's pure back-office with no presentation element will drain you. A relationship where you can't be playful and silly will go flat. A community where you can't do bits will bore you.

**What it helps you say no to:** Roles where you're invisible. Social contexts where performance is unwelcome. Anything that requires you to be permanently serious.

**What it helps you say yes to:** Presentations, teaching, facilitation with personality. Creative projects. Communities where humour is valued. The YouTube thing, or the next version of it.

**The tension to watch:** Performance can become people-pleasing. The Leo wants to shine; the Enneagram 3 wants to be valued. The check: _Am I performing because it's alive, or because I need the approval?_ When it's alive, it's effortless and fun. When it's approval-seeking, it's anxious and calculating.
### 3. Go deep with the few. Release the rest.

Your revealed preferences here are crystal clear. You have two close friends you voice-note with for an hour a day. You ignore most other messages. You want to declare message bankruptcy. You skip events you bought tickets to. You hide in your room — and then have incredibly deep, high-context conversations with chosen people.

This isn't introversion. It's selectivity. The Taurus moon wants depth and loyalty, not breadth.

**The decision test:** _Is this a deep investment in someone I've chosen, or a shallow obligation to someone I haven't?_

**What it helps you say no to:** The specific things on your Angle 3 list: shallow team meals, nightclubs, drowning in messages from people you don't want to talk to, aversive family messages answered out of guilt, emotional work practices done because "it seems cool" rather than because you need them.

**What it helps you say yes to:** The group chat. Family therapy (chosen, deep, ongoing[^2]). Circling when it's real. The Person 2 aspiration: one great partnership, deeply invested.

**What it helps you actually do:** Declare message bankruptcy. Communicate to the shallow-message tier: "I don't do messages anymore, let's email but we probably won't." You already wrote that you want to do this. This principle says: yes, do it, it's aligned, stop feeling guilty.

**The tension to watch:** "Deep with the few" can become "isolated with an excuse." You spent October 2025 to January 2026 at the EA Hotel barely connecting with anyone and rationalising it as introversion. The check: _Am I choosing depth, or am I avoiding the discomfort of opening up?_ If you're sitting in your room feeling lonely but not going downstairs, that's not this principle — that's the old pattern.

### 4. Follow the energy. Not the "should."

Consensus-ism, restated as a decision-making rule. The biography is the evidence: when you followed energy (Asia, Kensho, music, the scrapbook), things were alive. When you followed "should" (nightclubs, VIEW conversations, replying to messages out of obligation, spending time with family), things were dead and you knew it immediately.

**The decision test:** _Am I doing this because there's genuine energy here, or because I think I should?_ The body knows before the mind does. If you're buying a ticket and already dreading it, that's data.

**What it helps you say no to:** VIEW conversations done because "Joe Hudson is cool." Events bought out of FOMO. Gym sessions done alone out of obligation (you said it yourself: you need social accountability or it doesn't happen — so either find gym buddies or stop pretending solo gym is going to work). Replying to messages from obligation.

**What it helps you say yes to:** The thing that's pulling you. The project you can't stop thinking about. The person you actually want to talk to. The study session you'd do on a Saturday for fun.

**The tension to watch:** This is the most dangerous principle to misuse. "Follow the energy" can become "never do anything hard or boring." Some important things don't feel alive at first — they become alive through commitment. Person 2 didn't become a successful VC by following moment-to-moment energy; they stuck with something through the boring middle. The check: _Is the energy absent because this is genuinely dead, or because I haven't given it enough runway to come alive?_ Scrunching is forcing yourself to stay in something dead. But not everything that's temporarily boring is dead.

## What about "I don't need to earn my existence"?

I considered making this principle 5. It's real — it's the background project of your adult life, and when you lose hold of it, the other four principles all contort into striving.

But I don't think it's the same _kind_ of thing as the other four. The other four are decision-making tools: they help you choose between options on a Tuesday. "I don't need to earn my existence" is more like the ground they stand on. It's the difference between:

- _"I walk into chaos and make it legible **because that's my gift**"_ (healthy)
- _"I walk into chaos and make it legible **to prove I'm valuable**"_ (striving)

So I'd frame it not as principle 5, but as the **meta-check** you run on all four principles: _Am I doing this from aliveness, or from the need to justify myself?_
