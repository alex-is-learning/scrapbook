# Claude
You've done great thinking here already. And you're right — you spotted the key issue yourself: an argumentative essay needs a claim you're defending, not just a topic to explore.

Your instinct to add "Seeking for growth is the problem" was exactly the right move. That gives you something to argue _for_ (or against, or in a nuanced way). The difference between "==discuss X==" and "==X is true== — argue for or against" is basically the difference between an ==expository essay== and an ==argumentative one==.

> Useful distinction."Expository" was not a word in my brain

Here's what I'd suggest as your prompt to yourself:

**"The desire for self-improvement is itself the obstacle to self-improvement." Argue for or against this claim, drawing on Lao Tzu and Machiavelli.**

That works because it gives you a ==clear, debatable thesis to take a position on==, it connects naturally to what felt alive for you while reading, and it forces you to do real argumentative work — Lao Tzu and Machiavelli would seem to land quite differently on this, so you'll have to wrestle with the tension rather than just summarizing both.

As for what to prompt _me_ — now that you've read the material and have a topic, something like this would be a good starting point:

_"Here's my essay prompt: [prompt]. Here's what I think my thesis is, and here's my rough reasoning: [your thinking]. Can you push back on my argument, point out where it's weak, and help me figure out how to structure it? Don't write it for me."_

Basically: come to me with your _thinking_, not just the prompt. Even if it's messy and half-formed — that's the raw material. My job is to help you sharpen it, not replace it.
