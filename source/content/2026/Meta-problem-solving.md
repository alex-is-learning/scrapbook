Yes, this is genuinely meta-problem-solving, and I think you've named something real and useful. Let me push on it a bit because I think it's even more interesting than you're framing it.

**What you've identified:**

There's a regular problem ("my finances are a mess," "I'm not making progress on my project," "this relationship isn't working"), and then there's a _second-order_ problem about your _relationship to_ the regular problem ("I can't bring myself to look at it," "I don't know how to even think about this," "I freeze when I try to engage"). That second-order thing is the meta-problem. It's not the problem itself — it's the problem of _how you do problems_.

The recursive framing — "I don't touch my problems because I don't know how to touch my problems" — captures the loop really well. It's a problem about problem-having. And yes, that's textbook meta.

**Why I think this is sharper than just "meta":**

The meta-problem isn't just sitting one level up — it actively _prevents access_ to the level below. That's what makes it different from, say, "thinking about thinking," which is meta but not necessarily blocking. The meta-problem-solving you're describing is more like a _gate_ or a _bottleneck_: as long as it's there, no work on the underlying problem can happen. So it has a kind of operational urgency that pure metacognition doesn't.

This is also why it can feel so stuck and so shameful. You're not failing at the visible problem — you're failing at _being someone who can engage with problems_. That's an identity-level frustration, not a task-level one. And identity-level stuck-ness is famously hard to brute-force your way out of.

**The coaching observation is the most important part, I think:**

What you said about coaching "collapsing the meta-problem" is doing a lot of work. The way I'd put it: a good coach doesn't solve your problem and often doesn't even help you solve it directly. They give you the _capacity to be in contact with it_ — they help you become someone who can look at the thing, hold it, poke it. Once that's restored, the underlying problem often turns out to be quite tractable, or at least normally-hard rather than impossibly-hard.

So coaching (and therapy, and certain kinds of friendship, and journaling, and sometimes just a walk) functions at the meta level. It's not "here's how to fix your finances" — it's "here's how to become someone who can sit with your finances without dissociating." Once you have that, the finances become a regular problem again.

**One small refinement I'd offer:**

I'd distinguish _meta-problem_ (the thing — "I can't engage with my problems") from _meta-problem-solving_ (the activity — working on that thing). Your post sort of conflates them, which is fine for a personal note but worth separating if you want to develop the idea further. The coaching example is meta-problem-_solving_. The frozen state is the meta-_problem_.

**Where I'd push you:**

The really interesting question this opens up is: what _causes_ a meta-problem? Why do some people, in some seasons, lose the ability to touch their problems? Some hypotheses worth turning over:

- The problems have piled up enough that approaching any one feels like approaching all of them.
- Past attempts to engage went badly and the nervous system learned that "looking at problems" = pain.
- The person doesn't have a trusted internal process for what to do once they've looked, so looking feels pointless.
- There's no external container (person, structure, ritual) that makes engaging feel survivable.

Coaching probably works because it addresses several of these at once — it provides the container, it limits the scope ("just this one for this hour"), and it offers a process so looking isn't dead-ended.

Anyway: yes, this is meta-problem-solving, and I think you're onto something worth keeping. The framing is clean and the coaching insight is the real payload.
