# The imaginary examiner

My reading: you sometimes grant another person the authority to decide what the situation means, and what it says about you.

The hidden step seems to be **promotion from participant to examiner**. Their knowledge, confidence, or social ease becomes a general right to judge.

“They seem assured.” → “They know the correct way to be.” → “Their reading outranks mine.”

These five concepts separate those steps. The formal terms belong to the named authors or fields. The short recall phrases are my suggestions.

## 1. The imaginary examiner

*Lacan · subject supposed to know*

You can assign someone the position of “the one who knows” before you establish what they know.

Lacan developed this concept in his account of psychoanalytic transference. The analyst occupies a position of supposed knowledge for the analysand.

My extension to your example: the impressive person seems to possess an answer to how life works. You feel like the person who lacks it. Their authority comes partly from the position you assign them.

This closely fits your repeated surprise that impressive people do not have everything resolved. The surprise suggests that you granted them that status first.

“Imaginary” refers to the comprehensive authority you infer. Other people can have real knowledge and real power.

**Recall question:** “What have I assumed they know?”

A psychoanalytic concept, used here as an interpretive analogy. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Lacan, section 2.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lacan/#OthOedComSex).

## 2. Expert in what?

*Social psychology · halo effect*

An impressive person can seem correct across subjects that require different evidence and skills.

The halo effect concerns the influence of an overall impression on judgements of particular qualities. Nisbett and Wilson demonstrated this with different presentations of the same instructor.

Your history professor can know much more history than you. That gives you a reason to trust relevant historical claims. It does not establish their judgement about your character, relationships, or life priorities.

My application: you seem to convert a specific strength into general superiority. **Competence is local** is a useful phrase for that conversion.

The point is to give expertise an appropriate scope. You can learn from someone without treating every opinion as expert judgement.

**Recall question:** “What is their expertise relevant to here?”

Experimental research supports the general effect. This study does not establish the cause of your particular experience. [Nisbett and Wilson, 1977](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/92158/TheHaloEffect.pdf).

## 3. Familiarity with the room

*Bourdieu · embodied cultural capital*

A person can inherit familiarity with a social setting, then experience that familiarity as natural competence.

Embodied cultural capital includes dispositions and capacities that people acquire over time. Family life and education distribute these advantages unequally. Bourdieu also describes how acquired advantages can appear to be natural gifts.

This gives your class and education thread a concrete explanation. Some people receive years of practice with the language, references, and conduct that a particular group rewards.

My application: their ease can mean “this environment is familiar to me”. You can mistakenly read it as “I understand reality better than you”.

The advantage is real. Its existence does not establish greater human worth or comprehensive wisdom. Nor does it prove that the confident person is a fraud.

**Recall phrase:** “They know this room.”

A sociological account of learned advantage. [Bourdieu, The Forms of Capital, 1986](https://web.stanford.edu/~eckert/Courses/ParisPapers/Bourdieu1986.pdf).

## 4. Between us

*De Jaegher and Di Paolo · participatory sense-making*

The interaction itself can help explain the awkwardness.

Participatory sense-making treats social understanding as something people shape together through interaction. The interaction can develop patterns that neither participant intends.

This fits your dinner example. Imagine that you sense reserve, become cautious, and ask fewer questions. The other person reads your caution as disinterest and becomes more reserved. This is an illustration, not a claim about what happened.

“I am awkward” turns an event into a personal property. “We did not find a comfortable rhythm” preserves the role of both people and the circumstances.

Shared causation does not mean equal responsibility. One person can contribute much more to a difficult exchange.

**Recall question:** “What is happening between us?”

A theoretical approach to social cognition. [De Jaegher and Di Paolo, 2007](https://hannedejaegher.net/wp-content/uploads/2009/11/dejaegherdipaolo07participatorysensemaking.pdf).

## 5. Judgement without guarantees

*Pragmatist philosophy · fallibilism*

You can have good reasons for a judgement and remain open to correction.

Fallibilism rejects the requirement that a belief must be immune to error before it can guide inquiry or action. Pragmatists combine this openness with confidence in inquiry.

You already see that other people lack final certainty. This concept adds permission to use your own judgement under the same conditions.

You do not need to prove that the professor is confused before your discomfort counts. You can respect their expertise and conclude that the conversation did not suit you.

**Shared fallibility does not imply equal expertise, identical experiences, or the absence of truth.** The evidence still matters. You can revise your view without surrendering your right to form one.

**Recall phrase:** “I can judge, and I can revise.”

A philosophical position, not a claim that all opinions have equal merit. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Pragmatism, section 4.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/#SkepVersFall).

## Where emptiness enters

My proposed connection to your retreat: examine the apparent solidity of the **judge–judged relationship**.

“They are right and I am deficient” can feel like a fact that exists independently of the encounter. But your description contains several contributions: their presentation, your expectations, social conventions, and the exchange itself.

Their judgement also has conditions: their history, interests, knowledge, and current perspective. It can contain accurate feedback without becoming a complete account of you.

> “They have a perspective. I have a perspective. We can both learn something here.”

This is an application to your essay, not an equivalence between these theories and Mahamudra. A judgement can be conditional and still have real consequences.

## The revealing exception

Your draft says: “Unless they're someone like David Chapman, maybe. Or Dan Brown.”

This looks like the same pattern in a smaller group: most people lose their special status, while a few retain it.

You can value their insight without treating them as exceptions to the need for evidence and scope. The useful change concerns the authority you grant a person.

Your anger at confused people also deserves that distinction. You can reject a harmful claim without reducing its author to “a confused person”.

One possible further need is reliable support. If part of the wish for “adults” is a wish for care, an account of uncertainty will not meet that need alone. A reliable person can offer care without possessing final answers.
