Alex and Dave

  • (Initialising the project with the slash command /dave init)

Full Transcript

Dave: Scaffolding is ready. Before we go further, I need to calibrate where you actually are — not where you think you are, not where you’d like to be. I’ll ask three things. Answer honestly, not impressively.

What do you currently know about schema therapy? Don’t tell me what you’ve heard of — tell me what you can actually explain or do with it right now.

Alex: I currently don’t know all that much at all, I’ve read a few pages of a primer doc, I know that there are 18 things, I forget the name but they’re like, maladaptive schemas. I think I have 12 of them! And I know there’s some ontology to the theory, some directionality and groupings, but yeah, couldn’t tell you much more than that right now! I know that it was developed in the 1980s by a guy with a PhD (don’t know much about his background, presumably a therapist?). He saw that CBT doesn’t work with people who have deeper patternings. The idea is that core wounds in childhood lead to certain patterns of behaviour to soothe these wounds, I think, but it’s definitely more nuanced/high resolution than that. And it seems similar to Internal Family Systems and Coherence Therapy, insofar as the whole “you’re doing stuff for adaptive, coherent but now-maladaptive reasons”

Dave: You mentioned you have 12 of these schemas. Name one of them and tell me: what does it mean, concretely, to have that schema?

Alex: Oops! One that I remember is about being punitive. This is reminding me that there were I believe 5 or 6 categories of schema (that is, of the 18), and 4 of them really resonated with me, and two of them didn’t. I want to learn the names of these 6 and the 18, the ontology of this theory, the axioms. But anyway, being punitive - patterning of being really quite harsh, to others and yourself. Having exacting standards, etc. This resonated for me very hard. I’ve especially noticed this patterning after meditation retreats, when my shamatha has been heightened, but it’s always there.

Dave: You’ve described what Punitiveness looks like — the harsh behaviour, the exacting standards. But you didn’t answer what it means to have that schema. I’ll note that gap. We’ll return to it.

What do you want to be able to do or understand by the end of this? Be concrete — “understand it better” is not a goal.

Alex: I think to “have” a schema is to behave downstream of this stuff seeming true to you, adaptive, the way to act. I imagine that the brain is full of schemas, of models for how to act, and this theory describes 18 maladaptive ones. And they were probably adaptive when you were a child, but are now holding you back as an adult.

By the end of all this, I would like to have a fluent understanding of this theory such that I can interact with it as a therapeutic modality. The ultimate goal is to “refactor” these schemas, to outgrow them, to replace them. I imagine schema therapy also includes methods for schema “replacement” (memory consolidation is one method-of-action that Dr Bruce Ecker of Coherence Therapy talks about). So, rather than flying blind and doing the therapy with no theoretical underpining, I want to learn the theoretical underpining (efficiently!) to empower me to engage with the therapy, and also to be able to talk to others about it in a non-muddled way. To have a logically valid model of the therapy in my brain, basically. To grok it and benefit from it.

Dave: That’s a concrete goal. Now — what’s your timeline? How long do you have, and how much time per week are you putting in?

Alex: This is currently my full-time goal. Ideally, I’d like to have a concrete and fluent understanding of this theory in a week or less, maybe 3 hours a day. I doubt it’s a complex model, it’s not like learning math where there are many prerequisites. I doubt there are many axioms that I need to learn, basically!

Dave: Good. I have what I need.

The notes/ folder is where you write between sessions — but not reminders to yourself. Arguments to me.

Write as if you’re making a case to someone who will challenge every vague claim. Not “remember that X works by…” — instead, “X works by Y because Z, and this matters because…” I read notes as a rigorous reader, not a sympathetic one. If you write something vague, I’ll treat it as a gap, not a note.

You don’t have to write anything yet. But that’s the standard when you do.

Do you have source material you want me to use as the basis for your primer — a book, paper, article, or notes? If yes, paste or reference it now. If no, I’ll research the topic independently.

Alex: I have put two pdfs in the resources folder, you should be able to see them.

Dave: [Read both PDFs: schema-therapy-primer.pdf (13pp) and Schema Therapy: A Practitioner’s Guide (Young, Klosko & Weishaar, 2003). Used primer doc as primary source.]

I’ve written dave-primer-test-topic.md. Please read it and let me know if anything is wrong, missing, or pitched at the wrong level.

A note worth flagging: your assumption that “there aren’t many axioms” is worth testing. The schema taxonomy is relatively flat (18 schemas, 5 domains), but once you layer in the three coping styles and the mode model, the number of interacting structures grows considerably. The mode model alone has ~10 named modes. This doesn’t make it hard — your IFS and Coherence Therapy background means much of it will click quickly — but the architecture is richer than a flat list suggests.

Once you’re satisfied with the primer, we’re done with init. Run /dave hello to start your first session.
